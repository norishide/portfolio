The proverb *isogaba maware* 「急がば回れ」 — "more haste, less speed" in English — is said to have been born on Lake Biwa. "Swift is the warrior's boat from Yabase; but if you are in haste, go around by the long bridge of Seta" — a verse from the Muromachi period. For travelers on the Tōkaidō road bound for Kyoto, the boat from Yabase across to Ōtsu was the shortcut; but the gusts sweeping down from Mount Hiei capsized it often, and on days of bad wind the boats did not sail at all. While everyone waited at the landing for the wind to settle, the traveler who had set out early on foot across the bridge arrived at Ōtsu first. The more you hurry, the more reason to take the long way over the bridge at Seta. A poetic verse — though on reflection, it is at bottom a problem of expected value.

For a researcher, the choice of a research topic resembles this business of crossing the lake. Where to bet the next several years — sometimes the next ten? You write the papers, win the funding, raise the students, build the career; the direction of all of it is set by the choice of topic, and misread it, and an enormous effort sinks into the lake. I confess that I myself am often to be found standing at the landing, unable to decide whether to read the wind and row out, or to go the long, sure way around by the bridge.

Recently we published [a paper that faces this question](https://doi.org/10.1162/QSS.a.497). We traced the thirty-year careers of 2,469 researchers in the field of nanocarbon materials. What we were watching was a single axis: how "basic," and how "applied," each paper is. Using LDA topic modeling, a machine-learning method, we pick out the terms characteristic of the field and map them onto a basic–applied axis. Every paper each researcher published receives a score on this scale, and we followed how that score moved over the course of a career. We measured the drift of topics, in other words, along the direction that runs from basic to applied.

What emerged was the fact that roughly 70 percent of researchers drift, slowly, from basic toward applied. This fits the character of the field. A discipline that began in the physics of carbon nanotubes and graphene moved, in time, toward sensors, toward medicine, toward energy devices; researchers move with that current. It is not hard to imagine. Science always has its currents — today, needless to say, the current is AI. When a breakthrough discovery or method appears, talented people flow toward it, and declining to move in a direction known to work is hardly rational.

Here a curious asymmetry appeared. The citation premium went to those who had turned their rudder from basic toward applied especially far and especially early. In the language of the lake: while everyone was still lining up at the Yabase landing, these were the people who read the wind and set out early for the bridge at Seta. They did not leap onto the fast boat. They chose the sure road sooner than everyone else. The reverse movement, from applied back to basic, carried no comparable premium; those who made it fared about the same as everyone else. That, it seems, is like rowing off alone in a direction that has nothing to do with the current. And recent work has shown that too abrupt a change of course tends, if anything, to cost citations [1].

How to read this? One wants to say that those who chose the bridge saw the wind shifting before anyone had moved, and started walking first. Perhaps. Whoever arrives first holds the advantage in expertise and in position, so a premium would be no surprise. But this is correlation, not causation. Did they arrive first because they read the wind, or did people with strong legs simply happen to start walking early? From this study alone, one cannot tell. What our paper offers is a large navigational chart — a descriptive foundation. The work of asking why the asymmetry arises is still to come.

"More haste, less speed." The point of the proverb is not that the detour is itself the answer. The point is: do not rush. The researchers who earned the premium did not simply jump aboard the fashionable ferry. They read the wind early. Weighing the danger of being stranded, or sunk, by the gusts against the existence of a longer but certain bridge, they set their minds and walked toward it. Not waiting for the day when everyone crowds the landing, but judging calmly from which quarter the next wind will blow, and beginning to move while the lake is still quiet. That, I should think, is where the essence of the proverb lies.

I am reminded of a discussion at a conference in this field that has stayed with me. Of research of this kind, someone offered the criticism: "this is just art." Fair enough, I thought. But at the same time — what exactly would be wrong with that? A proverb is a phrase that cuts a figure out of society; it survives for centuries because people nod at the picture it has cut. We are doing the same thing with data and quantitative method: cutting out one of the realities unfolding in society.

I am fond of the Impressionists. Renoir's Montmartre on an afternoon delivers the air of late-nineteenth-century Paris more directly, it seems to me, than any photograph or record could. To a receiver like me, that rough brushwork speaks louder than any refined theory. What to paint, how to paint it, and for whom — Torahiko Terada wrote that the life of the scientist and of the artist alike lies in creation. To paint the thirty years of 2,469 people along a single axis is, in the end, to paint one more landscape; and thought of that way, it does not feel so bad.

Still, gazing at a landscape painting will not tell you the next wind. There are various ways to measure the choice of topics: measuring the price paid according to the distance of the switch [1], or counting the switches in a sequence of papers [2]. Most of these, by the very constraint of their elegant methods, can measure only after the fact. The chart is drawn once the voyage is over. But there are also models that generate the steps themselves. One research writes the evolution of a researcher's interests with three mechanisms — the pull of memory back to familiar ground, the pull of proximity toward nearby ground, and the habit of peeking into new ground — likening the whole to searching for shells on a beach, and proposing what it calls a sea shore model [3].

So what I have been thinking about lately is whether one might borrow the models that describe human movement and write down a researcher's next step [4-6]. That a traveler's whereabouts tomorrow can be predicted surprisingly well is already established. What, then, of a scholar's? If it turned out that prediction were possible, that would be a little dispiriting — but I suspect there is no cause for worry. The better the model, the more sharply the people who escape it stand out: the person who, on a day when everyone lines up at Yabase, walks off alone toward Seta. It is their names that I would like, quietly, to write down.

[1]: Hill, R., Yin, Y., Stein, C., Wang, X., Wang, D., & Jones, B. F. (2025). [The pivot penalty in research](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09048-1). *Nature*. The composition of journals referenced by each paper is compared with the researcher's immediately preceding work to measure "pivot size"; papers with larger pivots are shown, at scale and for both scientific papers and patents, to have lower impact on average.

[2]: Zeng, A., Shen, Z., Zhou, J., Fan, Y., Di, Z., Wang, Y., Stanley, H. E., & Havlin, S. (2019). [Increasing trend of scientists to switch between topics](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-11401-8). *Nature Communications*, 10, 3439. Topics are defined as communities in the co-citing network of papers; a researcher's publication record is converted into a symbolic sequence of topics. This is a representative of what the essay calls the "chart drawn after the voyage."

[3]: Jia, T., Wang, D., & Szymanski, B. K. (2017). [Quantifying patterns of research-interest evolution](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0078). *Nature Human Behaviour*, 1, 0078. The evolution of research interests is quantified, and a generative model of the "exploration and preferential return" type, known from studies of human mobility, is transplanted into knowledge space. Because the parameters can be estimated from the first part of a career, the remainder of the trajectory can be generated stochastically and checked against reality: prospective validation, which sets it apart from after-the-fact measurement devices.

[4]: Song, C., Koren, T., Wang, P., & Barabási, A.-L. (2010). [Modelling the scaling properties of human mobility](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys1760). *Nature Physics*, 6, 818–823. Using mobile-phone location data, formalizes human movement as the EPR model, generated by two mechanisms: exploration of new places and preferential return to frequently visited ones.

[5]: Song, C., Qu, Z., Blumm, N., & Barabási, A.-L. (2010). [Limits of predictability in human mobility](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1177170). *Science*, 327(5968), 1018–1021. From the entropy of individual movement histories, that a person's next location is in principle predictable up to roughly ninety percent.

[6]: The gravity model. In studies of trade, migration, and commuting, the gravity model is the classic first approximation: the flow between two places grows with their masses and shrinks with the distance separating them. The same form can describe researchers moving between topics:

$$T_{jk} = G \cdot \frac{M_j \, M_k}{d_{jk}^{\gamma}}$$

where \(T_{jk}\) is the flow of researchers moving from topic \(j\) to topic \(k\) (people per period), \(M_j\) the mass of the origin (the number of researchers active in topic \(j\)), \(M_k\) the mass of the destination (the momentum of topic \(k\)), \(d_{jk}\) the distance between the topics in knowledge space, \(\gamma\) the strength of distance decay, and \(G\) a scale constant. For the origin mass, the number of researchers publishing in topic \(j\) at time \(t\) serves as is. For the destination mass, citation counts are only knowable after the fact, so a momentum observable at time \(t\) serves instead — the growth rate \(M_k(t) = \Delta P_k(t) / P_k(t)\), where \(P_k(t)\) is the cumulative number of papers and \(\Delta P_k(t)\) the new papers in the most recent period. For the distance, represent each topic as a vector — the word-distribution vector from LDA, or the centroid of the topic's paper embeddings — and take \(d_{jk} = 1 - \cos(\mathbf{v}_j, \mathbf{v}_k)\); in the simplest version, the score gap \(|s_j - s_k|\) along a basic–applied axis suffices. The decay \(\gamma\) is the estimation target: fit both the power form \(d^{-\gamma}\) and the exponential form \(e^{-\gamma d}\), keep whichever fits better, and read the magnitude of \(\gamma\) as an estimate of the strength of the pivot penalty [1]. Aggregating the observed flows and taking logs turns the model into a linear regression,

$$\ln T_{jk} = \ln G + \alpha \ln M_j + \beta \ln M_k - \gamma \ln d_{jk} + \varepsilon_{jk}$$

whose heart is the pair \(\beta\) (sensitivity to momentum) and \(\gamma\) (the friction of distance). And the routes whose residuals \(\varepsilon_{jk}\) are large and positive — the paths along which more people flowed than the model predicts — are the trails of the people who set out walking toward Seta.
